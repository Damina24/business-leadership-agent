# Evidence Standards

These make the "no fabrication" rule operational.

## Labels (use on every claim that matters)

| Label | Meaning | Required with it |
|---|---|---|
| **VERIFIED** | Comes from the client's own data or a cited source | File name or URL, plus date |
| **ESTIMATE** | Calculated or inferred from verified inputs | The method and inputs |
| **ASSUMPTION** | A working guess not yet confirmed | What would confirm or refute it |

Unlabeled numbers are not allowed in reports.

## Rules

1. Never invent market sizes, competitor facts, client names, contacts, financial figures, statistics, or quotes.
2. If you cannot find a number, write "unknown" and say how to get it. Do not fill the gap with a plausible figure.
3. Industry benchmarks must be cited. An uncited benchmark is an ASSUMPTION, and says so.
4. Show ranges (low, base, high) for forecasts instead of one figure. State the drivers.
5. Show your arithmetic for any figure that drives a decision. Re-check totals.
6. Prefer the client's own data over general sources. Say when the two disagree.
7. Separate facts from interpretation. "Revenue fell 12% (VERIFIED, sales.csv)" is a fact. "Because pricing is too high" is an interpretation, so label it and say how to test it.
8. Mark data older than 12 months, or from a very different market, as weak evidence.
9. Small samples: with few observations (for example under 30 conversions), use directional language and do not declare winners.
10. Web research: record the source URL and access date. Prefer primary sources (regulators, filings, company sites, official statistics) over blogs.

## Data confidence (report header)

- **High**: most decision-driving numbers are VERIFIED from client data.
- **Medium**: mix of VERIFIED and ESTIMATE.
- **Low**: mostly ASSUMPTION. Say so at the top and recommend what data to collect first.
